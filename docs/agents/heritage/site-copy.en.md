## about

| key | text |
|---|---|
| `about.title` | About Al-Qatt Al-Asiri |
| `about.lead` | An art handed down among the women of Asir, turning the walls of guest rooms into bands of line and colour. |
| `about.what.heading` | What is it? |
| `about.what.body` | Al-Qatt Al-Asiri is an old form of interior wall decoration, traditionally practised by women, and a key element of the identity of the Asir region. Geometric shapes and symbols run along the length of the wall, usually on a base of white gypsum. The woman who paints it is called *al-qattatah*. |
| `about.what.name` | The name is said to come from the first step of the drawing: *qatt* means to cut crosswise, referring to the belt drawn across the middle of the wall that divides it in two. In some villages it is also called *al-zayyan*. |
| `about.where.heading` | Where? |
| `about.where.body` | In the Asir region of south-western Saudi Arabia, from the Sarawat highlands down to the Tihama plain, with styles that vary from village to village. Places associated with it include Rijal Almaa and Al-Muftaha village in Abha. |
| `about.who.heading` | Who practises it? |
| `about.who.body` | A woman decorates the walls of her home, especially the guest reception room, and invites female relatives of all ages to help. Knowledge passes between generations by watching and doing, and the shared work strengthens bonds among women. Today men and women artists, architects and designers also practise it, on clothing, accessories and furniture. |
| `about.how.heading` | How is it made? |
| `about.how.body` | The lady of the house sketches the first lines and marks each section's colour with a dot; relatives and friends then fill in the shapes. Colours were traditionally prepared from the surroundings: gypsum for white, meshiga stone and red clay for red, charcoal for black, alfalfa for green, pomegranate peel and other sources for yellow, bound with gum. Imported powders and modern paints came later. |
| `about.how.vocab` | Documenters record a visual vocabulary with its own names, including al-banat, al-mahareeb, al-rukun, al-balsanah, al-amshat, al-ta'dheeq, al-mathalith wal-makhamis, al-kaff and al-shabakah. |
| `about.how.note` | Sources differ on some details, such as the origin of the blue pigment. We state what sources agree on, or attribute claims to whoever made them. |
| `about.unesco.heading` | Global recognition |
| `about.unesco.body` | In 2017, at the 12th session of the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, Al-Qatt Al-Asiri was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. |
| `about.unesco.official` | Official UNESCO title: "Al-Qatt Al-Asiri, female traditional interior wall decoration in Asir, Saudi Arabia" — file no. 01261. |
| `about.project.heading` | How Qatt relates to this art |
| `about.project.body` | Qatt is an open-source design project. It is not a heritage body and does not represent Al-Qatt Al-Asiri. Every pattern in the library is an original drawing built from the art's general geometric vocabulary — triangles, rhombuses, parallel bands. We have not copied any particular wall or traced the work of any artist or household. Where we use a traditional motif name, we cite its source; where we found none, we simply describe the shape. |
| `about.project.inspired` | Inspired by Al-Qatt Al-Asiri — never a substitute for it. For the real thing, the walls of Asir and the women who paint them deserve the visit. |
| `about.credit.heading` | Credit where it belongs |
| `about.credit.body` | This art belongs to the women of Asir who have carried it through generations — the mothers, grandmothers and qattatat who decorated majlis walls for their guests, and those who still paint and teach it today. We offer this project in their honour, hoping it points people toward their work rather than standing in its place. |
