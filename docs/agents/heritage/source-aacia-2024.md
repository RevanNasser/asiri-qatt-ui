## Nature of the source (how much weight it carries)

- This is a **secondary synthesis**. Most of the heritage content (pp. 290–297) restates earlier theses and articles. The main ones are عارف 2016 (*International Design Journal*), القحطاني 2019, الدعجاني 2016 (PhD, Umm Al-Qura), الشهراني 2008, زكريا 2019, the Maghawi 2004 typology, and Mauger 2009. Some statements are attributed to practitioners: the artist **Zahra al-Almaʿi** (زهرة الألمعي) and **Khiyar al-Shahri** (خيار الشهري). The paper does not say whether these are the author's own interviews or quotes taken from the cited works. Treat them as **reported oral testimony**.
- The paper's own primary data is an online questionnaire (n = 111: 77 women, 34 men, p. 298). It measures opinions about identity and design reuse, not facts about the art. Do not cite it as evidence about motifs or history.
- The paper contains **at least one factual error**: it dates the UNESCO inscription to "January 2018" (p. 297). UNESCO's own decision is 12.COM 11.B.27, from December 2017 [S2, S3]. Keep 2017.
- It also contradicts itself on animate forms. It says the art is "free of animate forms" (p. 286), then reports birds in Qahtan decoration (pp. 290, 301).
- **Weighting rule for us:** a claim supported only by this paper is `verified, secondary [S14, p. N]`. Interpretive or symbolic readings it offers ("كأنها", "ربما", "قد") stay **interpretation**, attributed to the paper.

## Structured summary

1. **Aim and method (pp. 285–288):** it examines how Qatt motifs and colours express the social-cultural identity of the southern region. It uses an inductive and analytical method plus the questionnaire. The paper's angle is interior and product design.
2. **Region and practice (p. 290):** decoration is learned from mothers or from a gifted woman. It is "collective more than individual" (Mauger 2009). No date of origin can be fixed. Practitioners say they inherited it and do not know who began it.
3. **Unit typology and wall structure (pp. 291–295):** this is the core value for us. It covers the three-zone wall, the named bands (البترة، العماريات/التقطيعات، الحظية، الختام، الأرياش), the order of the bands, regional synonyms, and a 10-motif vocabulary table with descriptions and illustrations (p. 296).
4. **Design elements (pp. 292–294):** point, line and shape. It describes how black outlines are used, what horizontal and vertical lines are said to mean, and how lines turn at doors and windows.
5. **Colours and pigments (pp. 296–297):** primaries and secondaries, each with its traditional preparation.
6. **Heritage and identity (pp. 297–298):** general statements about tangible and intangible heritage, and recommendations, including "an electronic library of these motifs" (p. 304).
7. **Survey results (pp. 299–301):** 95.5% agree that Qatt expresses regional identity, and 98.2% agree that new designs can be derived from it.

### Wall structure and composition
| # | Claim | Page |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | A room's wall is divided into **three zones**: (1) the upper part is left **empty**; (2) the middle, **at eye level**, carries thin, medium and wide bands built from الأرياش، البنات، الختام، الحظية، المثالث، المخامس and العماريات; (3) the lower part has parallel lines (vertical or horizontal) or a **solid colour field** along the lower walls. | 291 |
| C2 | Band order, from Figure 4 and the text (top → bottom): **الأرياش** (banat triangles with plants) → **الختام** (a row of decorated squares) → **الحظية** (a narrow band) → thin rules → **العماريات** (vertical multicoloured stripes, the base). The text adds that the **حظية is always below the ختام**, with **banat and amshat above** it. | 295 |
| C3 | The **ختام** is 30–40 cm high. Its squares are filled with **الأرياش، المحاريب، البلسنة، المثالث والمخامس**, and the name derives from «ختمه». Some owners settle for khatam squares alone. | 295 |
| C4 | The **حظية** runs the length of the wall, is much thinner than the ختام, and is usually found in interior majlis rooms. | 295 |
| C5 | The **عماريات/التقطيعات** are close vertical parallel stripes. They are "the base of the painting, from which the artist's imagination rises to the حظية or ختام". They are vertical in Rijal Almaa and horizontal in Bilad Shahran, with **no more than seven** lines. | 293, 295 |
| C6 | The **البترة** is the central pillar that holds the main roof beam. Its length sets the length of the sitting room, so it is a sign of wealth. It is painted like a large carpet holding every motif type. In Bani Shahran a batra is rare, and a broad-lined **«محراب»** in the middle of the wall replaces it (Khiyar al-Shahri). | 291, 295 |
| C7 | The artist first draws **main straight black lines**, horizontal and vertical. Secondary lines and colour fields fill the spaces between them, mostly in red, blue, green and yellow. The work is strictly **two-dimensional**, with no depth. | 292 |
| C8 | **Black outlines** separate the colour fields so that each shape is clearly defined. | 294 |
| C9 | Painting proceeds **from top to bottom** (Zahra al-Almaʿi, reported). | 292 |
| C10 | Horizontal lines are the most common. Some houses have *only* horizontal lines, and their number varies with the owner's status. Together they form a **belt around the room**. At doors and windows the lines **turn vertical, then horizontal again**, and the same happens beside stairs. | 292, 294–295 |
| C11 | Decoration is **denser and more varied in well-off homes**, and sparser in modest ones (Zahra al-Almaʿi). | 290, 301 |
| C12 | Wall surfaces are divided in proportion to the wall's area and orientation. The wall facing arriving guests, or the head wall of the coffee room, gets a complete design "like a carpet or a frame". | 291 |
| C13 | Shapes include equilateral triangles, vertical or horizontal rhombuses and opposed triangles. Squares and rectangles are filled with diagonal or orthogonal grid lines. Symbols include geometric stars, polygons, trees and roses. | 293 |

### Motifs (the p. 296 table, with p. 292–295 text)
| Motif | What the paper says | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **البنات (البناه)** | Usually at the **top** of the painting, on the batra, wall, khatam or hadhiyya. A **tree resting on an upward-pointing triangle**. The illustration shows each triangle topped by a sprig with **several branch pairs**. *Interpretation (paper):* "as if representing lined-up mountains". | 296 |
| **الأرياش** | "Come in the form of **plants between the banat**." *Interpretation (paper, hedged «ربما»):* this may reflect a link between the female and the plant as elements of fertility. | **المحاريب** | "Broken lines **across** [horizontal], in the shape of successive **sea waves**." The illustration shows **stacked multicoloured horizontal zigzags**. *Interpretation (paper, hedged «قد»):* this may resemble rows of worshippers facing the mihrab. | 293, 296 |
| **سنكرولي** | "Broken lines **lengthwise** [vertical], **facing right** (usually)." It is "a form of mahareeb set side by side in parallel". The illustration shows vertical multicoloured **chevron columns**. | 293, 296 |

| **البلسنة** | Rhombus **moulds** with dots or small circles inside, usually in the hadhiyya or khatam. *Etymology (Khiyar al-Shahri via Aref 2016):* named after **lentil grains, locally called «البلسن»**. | 292, 296 |

### Colours and preparation (pp. 296–297)
| # | Claim | Page |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | The primaries **red, yellow and blue** are the main colours. **Green and orange** are added as secondaries. | 296 |
| P2 | **Red:** made from «حسن»، **مرّ** (myrrh?) and **fried, finely ground rice**. The rice "softens the intensity of the «حسن» colour". *«حسن» is unidentified, possibly a local name for a red earth or stone. Record it verbatim.* | 297 |
| P3 | **Green:** extracted from green plants. It is "one of the most important colours in the region". | 297 |
| P4 | **Blue:** made from **«طلح أبيض» (white acacia) and «زرنيخ أزرق» ("blue arsenic"; the mineral is unidentified)**. | 297 |
| P5 | **Black:** **hearth charcoal plus black gum** (صمغ أسود). | 297 |
| P6 | **Yellow:** **natural sulphur powder or pomegranate peel**. | 297 |
| P7 | **Orange:** **brought from Yemen as a powder** (Maghawi 2004). | 297 |
| P8 | **Tools:** brushes made of **goat hair**, and «المطاط» for taking measurements (meaning unclear: rubber or elastic cord). An earlier source says sheep tails were used as brushes. | 294, 296 |

### Social role and identity
| # | Claim | Page |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Women excelled at the art. The popular artist (القطّاطة) places colours and motifs according to her own instinct and aesthetic, in line with her community's outlook, so the whole community embraces her work (Mauger 2009). | 286 |
| S2 | It is an independent art with its own social meaning (Qurban 2020). Its colours are described poetically as "the whiteness of hearts, the colours of roses and the pigments of mountain soil", and its lines as "the meanders of valleys". *(This is poetic description, not evidence.)* | 290 |
| S3 | *Interpretation (Zakaria 2019, via the paper):* horizontal lines come from **agricultural terraces** and vertical lines from **mountains**. Horizontal lines are said to carry "stillness, balance, stability", and vertical lines "ascent, firmness". | 290, 292, 294 |
| S4 | The contemporary artist **Zahra al-Almaʿi** took part in an **18 m mural in New York titled «بيوت أمهاتنا»**. | 290 (footnote) |
